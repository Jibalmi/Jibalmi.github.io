One queue, every request with its exact command.

A print, a firmware flash, a click an agent asks for on your desktop, a patch to the agents themselves: each files an approval and waits. You decide at your desk or on your phone, and the agent hears your answer.

Each request names its risk, its command and who asked.

Each request carries its risk, its command and the agent that asked. Deny is one tap; a print or a flash needs your written reason.

Risky steps wait on your phone.

Pair your phone with a QR code on your network. Each approval arrives on it with the request and its risk level. The phone’s own routes list and resolve approvals through the same logic as the desk; an approval answered on a physical phone is not yet proven.

Decide from anywhere in the house.

Low risk takes one tap. Anything higher asks for your passkey, or waits for your desk. Deny is always one tap.

Approvals

Stop, steer, hand off.

What you can do to a running session or its task, each row with the route it calls.

  1. pause

    The terminal host suspends the process tree; the row says paused only when the pause took effect. Resume continues it.

    sessions/[id]/pause

  2. hand off

    The session writes its handoff, and a fresh one continues from it.

    sessions/[id]/handoff

  3. restart

    Start a session that has gone wrong again from its brief, or end it.

    sessions/[id]/restart

  4. reassign

    Move a task to another agent, from the task’s own menu.

    tasks/[id]

Approvals

Why did the agent do that?

Decisions are attributed as they are made, with the facts, skills and analogues they used, in a chain you can query afterwards.

The model router, the spawn plan, the voice loop, the channel’s ingest, skill executions, approvals and session hand-offs are wrapped in the attribution recorder. Ask for a task’s chain with whyDidAgentDoX from an agent, or GET /api/attribution/<taskId>.

Code an agent writes for the CAD kernel runs in a jail: a Docker container with no network, a read-only root, every capability dropped, 2 GB of memory and 128 processes. The device bridge is disarmed: a flash, jog or torque command returns the exact command it would have sent, with executed: false, unless you arm it yourself.
